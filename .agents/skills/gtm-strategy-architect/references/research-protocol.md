# Research Protocol

## Purpose

Ensure research informs a specific strategic decision. Research is not a collection exercise and should not be used to decorate an already chosen answer.

## 1. Frame the decision

Before researching, state:
- The decision to inform.
- Why it matters now.
- Which alternatives are being considered.
- What evidence would change the recommendation.
- What level of confidence is required.

## 2. Define the evidence need

Choose the relevant evidence type:
- Market size, growth, and category dynamics.
- Competitor capabilities, positioning, pricing, and distribution.
- Customer problems, jobs, workflows, and buying behavior.
- Product usage, activation, retention, and value realization.
- Willingness to pay, budget, and procurement.
- Channel economics and funnel performance.
- Regulatory, security, and external constraints.

## 3. Source hierarchy

Prefer evidence appropriate to the claim:
1. Primary data: product analytics, CRM, financials, experiments, customer interviews, contracts, win/loss.
2. Official sources: company pricing/product pages, filings, regulator publications, standards, government statistics.
3. Reputable research: transparent methodology, sample, date, and definitions.
4. Expert and practitioner sources: useful for interpretation, but assess incentives and scope.
5. Secondary commentary and community discussion: useful for discovery and hypotheses, not automatically representative.

No source type is universally sufficient. A customer interview is strong evidence of that customer's experience, not necessarily market prevalence.

## 4. Assess source quality

For each important source, record:
- Publisher / author.
- Publication or collection date.
- Geography and population.
- Method and sample, where available.
- Definition of key metrics.
- Potential bias or commercial incentive.
- Relevance to the target segment and decision.
- Limitations.

Use the newest source available for fast-changing facts, but do not discard older primary evidence that remains directly relevant.

## 5. Synthesize without overclaiming

Separate:
- **Observed fact:** what the source directly reports.
- **Interpretation:** what the fact may imply.
- **Assumption:** what is being taken as true without sufficient evidence.
- **Recommendation:** what action follows, given the evidence and trade-offs.

Triangulate material claims where practical. Preserve conflicting evidence and explain why sources differ.

## 6. Market sizing protocol

For TAM/SAM/SOM:
- Define category, geography, customer, use case, and time horizon.
- Prefer bottom-up estimates when customer counts and realistic spend can be established.
- Show formulas, inputs, sources, and ranges.
- Avoid treating broad analyst categories as the addressable market.
- Distinguish total theoretical demand from reachable and serviceable demand.
- State uncertainty and sensitivity to assumptions.

## 7. Customer research protocol

For interviews and qualitative research:
- Recruit participants matching explicit segment criteria.
- Ask about past behavior and concrete workflows, not only hypothetical intent.
- Explore triggers, alternatives, consequences, budget, buying roles, and objections.
- Avoid leading questions and confirmation bias.
- Record sample size and recruitment limitations.
- Do not infer prevalence from a small convenience sample.

## 8. Pricing and willingness-to-pay

Combine methods where possible:
- Existing purchase and renewal behavior.
- Budget and procurement evidence.
- Competitor and alternative price points.
- Van Westendorp or Gabor-Granger when suitable to the research question.
- Value metric and cost-to-serve analysis.
- Controlled price/packaging tests where feasible.

Stated willingness to pay is not the same as actual purchase behavior.

## 9. Experiment design

A useful experiment specifies:
- Hypothesis.
- Riskiest assumption.
- Target population.
- Intervention or test.
- Primary metric and guardrails.
- Baseline and success threshold.
- Duration / sample rationale.
- Decision rule for positive, negative, or inconclusive results.
- Owner and follow-up.

Avoid A/B tests when sample size, randomization, or exposure conditions cannot support valid interpretation.

## 10. Evidence status

Use the standard statuses:
- **Validated:** directly and sufficiently evidenced for the decision.
- **Supported:** credible evidence exists but remains incomplete.
- **Hypothesis:** plausible, not yet established.
- **Unknown:** no adequate evidence.

Status is claim-specific, not document-specific. A single block may contain all four statuses.

## 11. Reporting

Every research summary should include:
- Decision question.
- Key findings.
- Evidence and source references.
- Confidence and limitations.
- Contradictory signals.
- Implications for the Canvas.
- Recommended next action.
